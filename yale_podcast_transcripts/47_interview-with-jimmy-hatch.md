HANNAH: Jimmy, you don’t simply have an amazing story to share, which we could, I’m sure, spend hours and hours on, but we think you have a very special perspective on the meaning of a college education and an appreciation for a lot of the values that we try to highlight on our podcast, like having wide ranging curiosity, a diversity of experiences, being open to learning from others, and willing to reflect on what it means to be a thinking, feeling, imperfect, dynamic, human.

JIMMY HATCH: Thank you for having me. I hope I earn my sandwich today.

HANNAH: I’m sure you will. All right. So we have a few questions that we wanted to get into with you. First of all, most of our listeners are students that are reaching the end of high school. For them, enrolling in college right away en route to a career that requires a college degree is probably the only future that they can imagine, or what they’ve always sort of pictured for themselves. That’s not where your head was at in high school. Do you want to tell us a little bit about your decision to join the military when you were 17 and the future you imagined for yourself?

JIMMY HATCH: I came up in a different kind of a family environment where I lived in different family situations, and some of them were less than optimal. And so actually, most of the relationships I had with human beings were transactional. If you act a certain way, you get to eat. If you act another way, you probably get a beating. I was drawn to books on the military, and particularly war, because it seemed there was no way to shroud the truth about love and relationships.

In combat, people would risk their lives for one another. And that was a litmus test that I felt was significant. So the military, being a transactional type of organization, show up on time, shine your boots, have a shave, you’re doing a good job. I was like, hey, man, I’m done with that. So it seemed like a really good idea for me. And college really was never on my radar. I didn’t ever believe that I had the intellectual capacity nor the patience to sit down and study. And honestly, because of the environment I grew up in, I didn’t really see any utility in it for me.

MARK: You were, however, a reader. Once, while you were deployed, you read a book by legendary Yale humanities professor, Harold Bloom. How did you come across his work, and why did his writing appeal to you? And specifically, why did it appeal to you when you were in that space?

JIMMY HATCH: Having been raised in many ways by the military, I would look at the people that I worked with, that I had respect for, and I would try to figure out what it is they were reading. Because I thought maybe if they exposed the things that they were taking in, it would help me become a better human. And I knew that the classics were important, and I knew that Shakespeare was important, and I knew that Cervantes was important, but I didn’t really know why.

And so I was getting ready to go on the deployment in 2005 and walking around a Barnes & Noble in Norfolk, Virginia, and I saw this book called _Genius_ by a guy named Harold Bloom. And in it, he talks about all of those folks that I’d mentioned and a bunch more. And it’s a pretty good-sized book. And so I’m like, OK, I only take one book on deployment. And several months later, we were involved in a really sad, kind of a tragic mission that they’ve made a movie out of now, called _Lone_ _Survivor._

And I remember, coming back down out of the mountains after that, it was a very difficult time. A lot of dead friends, a lot of suffering, it was a really dark period. And I was so drawn to the book because, of course, Professor Bloom wrote amazingly. And most of it was over my head, but I still felt like I was being taken for a ride and a good one, one that was leading me towards kind of light, something different than the darkness that I was experiencing.

And it was so compelling to me. I got out of my dirty clothes, and I cleaned my guns, and took a shower, and started reading. I fell asleep. The next morning, I was reading. And I got up and walked over to this little plywood shack that had our one unclassified computer in it. And I emailed him. His email address was in the back of the book. And I didn’t really even know where Yale was. It was so far from the galaxy that I inhabited that didn’t matter.

Anyway, I just wanted him to know how much that work had touched me and helped me in a specifically difficult time. Not a rough time, but carrying your dead friends and smelling them kind of time. And he wrote back one word, “survive.” And I was like, what a jerk, you know. It was before I realized that you smart Yale people try to say what you can say in as few words as possible. I’m still struggling with that, by the way.

HANNAH: Yeah, us too. Don’t worry. So you’ve been really open about struggles you’ve faced after being critically injured while you were on a mission. What helped you recover from that?

JIMMY HATCH: It’s interesting the way you asked that question, what helped you recover. Sometimes, I think people are considering their problems the way I looked at mine, insofar as if someone hasn’t had my experience, what’s the one thing that I can do? I’m listening to doctors. I’m listening to people. I’ve got my friends coming around. What is the one thing that got you over the edge? And there really is no one thing for me.

And I think it was a combination of both my colleagues, who were not social workers or psychiatrists, just coming and sharing space with me. And I didn’t like them to be there. And they didn’t know what to say. And I was pathetic. But the fact that they were there made me understand that they had to have some type of an affection for me or love for me.

They’d proved themselves on the battlefield, stopping me from bleeding to death. I’m laying there, screaming in a gunfight. And that, of course, was the litmus test for love that I came into the military with. And then here I am, pathetic, drunk, abusing drugs, ruining my relationships. And they just kept showing up. So that was, I think, the thing that initially sparked the rest of the process that I went through.

I don’t know that I would have done that. Because here’s the thing, man. I talk about mental health a lot, especially with law enforcement and veteran communities. At some point, you get to a place where you just don’t have hope. And there’s no prescription for hope. There’s a lot of medicines that will fix a lot of things, but I don’t know that there is anything that gives you hope, except for love. And that, I think, is a kind of word that gets bandied about often in the military or law enforcement.

Law enforcement communities, those guys love me, and they kept showing up. Those types of situations where people can be vulnerable and safe are important for humans in general. And I think we’ll probably talk about diversity a little bit, but I think that is another weight on the scale of diversity being a superpower, which I truly believe. And I would not have said that prior to Yale. But I have sat in classrooms with people who come from– professors, students who come from very different backgrounds.

And I traveled the world a fair amount. But here, it’s the same situation, different people, different cultures. But talking about things, at least I was, in the humanities, we were talking about things that were prescient from the time humans started recording history, Herodotus, to now. And it’s funny, as I kept going, I saw that those things really didn’t actually change a lot. We have iPhones and running water, but we’re still the same people who are capable of love and kindness and savagery and violence.

And diverse opinions make things harder to generalize. And that’s, I think, a gift, especially for someone like me who had some very strong opinions based on very strong experiences in very difficult circumstances.

MARK: So, Jimmy, before you came to Yale, you were already making news for a founding a nonprofit organization called Spikes K9 Fund. Tell our listeners a little bit about the organization and what moved you to start it.

JIMMY HATCH: Yeah. So my first dog that I worked with in combat was killed in Ramadi in 2006. I chased this guy and sent the dog after him. The guy was fighting the dog. And I put some bullets in him, and one of the bullets went through and killed my dog. He was my best friend, and he’d saved my life a bunch. So that was the first impact. And then I did more deployments with more dogs, some as a handler and some as a team leader.

And the night I got wounded, a dog got shot in the head about five feet in front of me and saved my life. That’s the only reason I knew there were bad guys there. When I came home after I got myself sorted out, I knew that I had to have a mission. And I was keen to go to the police and say, hey, my name is Jimmy Hatch, and I used to work with dogs in combat. And they’re like, yeah, we know who you are.

And sure, they didn’t need my help, but they knew I probably needed to have somebody put their arms around me and say, hey, come be part of our team, because that was such a big part of my life. And one of their dogs actually needed a surgery, and the municipality just didn’t have the resources or didn’t choose to send the resources that way. And I was absolutely taken aback that that was the case. This dog had been through some very tough things on behalf of the community.

As humans, we tell these dogs they don’t have a choice. They get to go with us. They don’t get a choice as to who they’re assigned to. And truth be told, I felt like that was a little bit of history as well. I didn’t have any choices about where I was born or who I ended up with and what they taught me or didn’t teach me. At any rate, because I felt– maybe I felt guilty. And maybe I had some ethical questions with regard to what we were doing, what we’re asking these dogs to do.

Because I tell you, man, if they knew what we were asking them to do, I think they would not do it. I really wanted to figure something out and help this dog and help the officer. Because the officer, when you have a relationship with a dog like that, they’re family in a big way, a bigger way sometimes than even the human family because they rely on you.

So I went to a friend who knew how to use this thing called Photoshop, and we made a logo out of a picture of Spike and I in Iraq about a week before he was killed. And we put some stars in it, and there’s four stars in it. And each one of those stars represent a dog that I served with in combat that was killed. And I put him on a sweatshirt, and I put it on Facebook and said, hey, I’m trying to raise money for a dog that needs a surgery.

Man, people love dogs. I made about I made about $6,000, which was enough to send the officer and the dog and get the procedure and come back. And I covered all of it. And I had a little money left over. So I was kind of off to the races, and it took off. We’ve helped well over 3,000 dogs in all 50 states with medical care, with the best ballistic vests. They’re custom-made for the dog, all kinds of equipment and medical stuff.

We want to make sure that we’re an advocate for those dogs. They don’t have a voice. And we want to be that voice that says they deserve to be cared for during the time they’re working for us humans. And then afterwards, when all of the– just like me, when I got out of the military, I had to go see the doctor a lot more because of the damage I sustained while doing the jobs that I did in the military. And the dogs are the same way.

So that was a mission for me. And I was grateful to have it because it gave me a direction to point my energies. And it also did something to me or for me that I didn’t know I needed. I did not feel as if I belonged in society. I felt as if I was somewhat of a monster because I enjoyed my job previous to getting hurt. And I always felt like if they knew the things that I’d done, if they knew what I was inside, they wouldn’t want to be around me.

But I had to get out and tell the story. I had to talk about myself, and I had to talk about the dogs in order to raise money and make the organization successful. So that takes a toll, however, and it’s another reason that I’ve stepped away from it. But it helped me become a better communicator. It helped me talk to people that I didn’t want to talk to. It helped me come out of my shell and see the society in a different way. And so for that, I’m extremely grateful.

It’s still out there. We’re really trying hard. There’s over 25,000 canine teams that are working daily here in the United States. And so there’s a big community of those souls out there helping us. And I want to make sure that they all have what they need.

HANNAH: Amazing. I think we’re going to pivot and start talking a little bit about your experience with Yale, starting with– and I just learned this, but you had a chance encounter with a Yale professor while you were skydiving, which kickstarted your interest in applying to Yale’s nontraditional student program. What do you remember about that conversation? And what do you think it was about you that made the professor encourage you to apply?

JIMMY HATCH: So a friend of mine, another SEAL, was at a conference and met Professor Zack Cooper. And we went skydiving. And first of all, this guy said, hey, man, it’s a Yale professor. I’m like, OK, some crunchy old dude’s going to come down. But he’s a young guy. He’s in great shape. He’s a good-looking dude. He looks like Bradley Cooper. Seriously, they confuse him all the time.

I was impressed with him because when we got out of the airplane– I mean, obviously he was nervous. But I had probably close to 6,000 jumps at that time. And I’d been an instructor in the military and in the civilian world. I’d seen a lot of people’s faces. I always say, when you jump out of the airplane for the first time, you can’t lie. I get to see your soul in your face. And he was committed and serene, which is great, because in the last few months, we went down to Florida together. And he got his skydiving license, and he’s kind of addicted to it now.

MARK: Oh, cool.

JIMMY HATCH: He messaged me and said, hey, maybe we could have you come up to Yale. And I was like, well, what the hell? OK. And I had been giving talks a fair amount. And he said, yeah, we do these things up here. They’re called a [? tea, ?] and it’s like a small kind of talk. And so I came up, and he walked me around. And he showed me Yale. I did my talk at TD. I actually stayed there. And that was the college that I ended up being in.

HANNAH: Oh, me too. [? Follow ?] TD. Yeah.

JIMMY HATCH: Nice. I didn’t realize what he was doing because I’m not that smart. He was showing me Yale, not as a, like, hey, check it out, look how cool it is, but as a, hey, you might think about spending some time up here as a student. And clearly, at that point, there was no way that I would have even considered it.

But anyway, two specific things happened that throttled me. The first one was he and I went to lunch. We’re sitting in TD, actually. You’re looking up, and these buildings are just crazy. Not posh, but they’re stately.

MARK: They’re spectacular, yeah.

JIMMY HATCH: And so it’s somewhat imposing for a guy like me who lived some of his life in a trailer park and slept on plywood over torpedoes and submarines. Like, this is a pretty big deal. And he mentioned something about me being a student at some point. And I said, Zack, I got no business being here. I mean, look at this. These kids, are you kidding me? I didn’t come from this. This isn’t my– this isn’t where I originated.

I don’t think he staged it, but man, he couldn’t have done a better job. There was a young woman sitting next to him at those big long tables that you can see eight people at. And she was sitting there. She had her lunch. And she had her computer open, and she was aggressively getting after both of them. And he said, pardon me, can we ask you some questions? And she said, yeah, sure.

He said, where are you from? And she said, I’m from Los Angeles, from Koreatown. He said, OK, what are your folks doing? She said, well, my father is unemployed, but my mother’s a waitress. And that was shocking. And then at some point, it became clear that she was flown to Los Angeles for– her parents, her family did not have the resources to have her go home on the holidays. And Yale helped her with that.

And I was really shocked at that. That is not what I thought a place like this would be. I thought it would be populated by kids who’d gone to really good schools, whose parents were very comfortable and could afford the tuition. And I had no real insight as to the way things work here. I left there. He didn’t really rub that in at the time. And then that night, we went to dinner with Jim Levinsohn, who’s the dean of the Jackson School, for whom I now work as a lecturer.

And he asked me at dinner about this tattoo I have on my forearm, and there’s some words in Spanish. And in 1971, when Pablo Neruda won the Nobel Prize for literature, in his banquet speech, he said, through blood and darkness, poetry is written. Poetry should be written. And that moved me, and I wanted to never forget that. And he said, Jimmy, you ought to apply for this program we have here called the Eli Whitney Student Program. And he claims I told him to F off, which I am completely capable of doing.

I don’t recall that. I recall laughing and rolling my eyes. And he just kind of dropped it, and we ended up talking about something else. So I left after that trip, went back to Virginia. And Professor Cooper sent me, Zack, he sent me– he said, hey, Jimmy, here’s the link to the Eli Whitney Students Program. Just check it out, see what you think. So I looked at it and laughed. And I didn’t really even understand some of the terms on it.

And then one day– I mean, he would text me every few weeks. And one day, I said, you know, what’s the worst thing that can happen? So I filled the application out. And I got the whole thing done in like 45 minutes. And I texted Zack, and I said, hey, man, I just want you to know I submitted my application. And he said, how long did it take you? I said, about 45 minutes.

And all of a sudden, my phone rings. And he’s like, Jimmy, wow. Wow. I said, yeah, it seemed like pretty straightforward, you know. And he said, well, did you do the essays? And I said, yeah. He goes, both of them? I said, mm-hmm. He said, how long did they take you? I said, about 15 minutes, I think. And he said, Jimmy, you don’t understand, man. Those essays, people hire people to get help with those because they’re so important. I’m like, well, it seemed pretty easy to me, man.

So he said, do me a favor and email Patricia Wei and ask her if you can resubmit the essays. And just have somebody go over them. It’s good to have someone check out your stuff. So I took the essays that I had written previously. And Zack and I went over them, and he helped me with this thing called punctuation and also grammar. But we really didn’t change them that much, which I thought was interesting.

And then that was it. I got an email that said I was accepted. And I was sitting at my dining room table, and my wife was sitting there. I said, well, it looks like I got into Yale. She looked at me, and she said, you are an idiot if you don’t take this opportunity.

MARK: So, Jimmy, one of the things that we try to encourage prospective students to do when they are going through the application process is to really just reflect and take a moment, and ask yourself, what is it that you want to get out of your college experience? We started off saying– for most of our students, they’ve never imagined a different world. You’ve lived in a different world.

At that moment, when you started, what did you want to get out of the experience? What was important to you? What were you looking for when you began that Yale journey?

JIMMY HATCH: I really didn’t know. I had talked to Professor Cooper, Zack, about this. And he said, Jimmy, some people come to college as a means of getting themselves to the next place they want to go in life. And some people come to college for consumption, and that’s OK. To me, that was a little bit unsettling. When you’re in the military, you have to think about the whole. You’re a lot more of a socialist than anything else. You have to think about the rest of the people in the unit.

And I thought, listen, man, if I’m coming up here, I’m taking a slot away from a young person. This might make the difference in their life. You know, what am I? And then one of the professors, when I got to Directed Studies, he helped me clarify what it was that made me want to be here. And he was going around the room the first day of class. And he looked at me, and he said, James, why are you here? What made you want to come here?

And I said, it just popped. It made sense. It was the clarifying statement. I said, I really want to be a better human. That was my motivation. And I would not have been able to tell you that initially. I didn’t know what I would get. And I spent a lot of time thinking I didn’t deserve to be here, which was a wasteful thing. And if any of you prospective students are in that frame of mind, before you get here or when you get here, I would tell you to stop it.

If these folks let you in, you need to get after it. And you need to not waste your time messing around, being insecure about why you’re here. That’s a useless question. The bottom line is you’re here. And the other people that are here are impressive, and you are impressive. And you need to get going and not waste a moment.

HANNAH: Looking back now, do you feel like your time at Yale majoring in the humanities in this liberal arts college environment helped teach you how to be a better human? Do you feel like you came out the other side of that and accomplished that goal to some extent?

JIMMY HATCH: Yeah, I think it’s an indulgence to judge yourself on your own human quality.

HANNAH: Well, sure.

JIMMY HATCH: But I’m not shy on indulgences. So I do, 100% And I think it changed me. And people ask me, well, what changed you? I think I had some very strong opinions before I came here on things like foreign affairs and even some of the cultural issues. And I hadn’t really ever studied anything. I felt as though my experiences were so powerful that I did have a podium to stand on and say, you’re wrong, this is what– but as I learned, specifically in Directed Studies, I didn’t have really any special insights on why war occurred.

I was OK at conducting it, and I committed myself in a very heavy way to it, but I didn’t really understand what it was going to solve, and I didn’t really understand the damage that the entirety of conflict causes for eons. And that was humbling. And it was a bumpy road for me. And I’m really grateful that my professors put up with me.

MARK: Another thing that we talk with our listeners a lot about is how admissions work fundamentally about building a community. I like to say that the 6,600 undergraduates at Yale are, in my mind, the most valuable learning resource that we can provide. What were some of the lessons that you learned just by being in community with your Yale classmates? And what do you think were some of the things that they learned by having you as a classmate?

JIMMY HATCH: When I talked to people outside of the university about my experiences, the first thing I often say is, look, I didn’t spend time with the veterans community. I didn’t spend my time with my fellow Eli Whitney classmates. I spent my time with my classmates in Directed Studies who were just out of high school, and it was absolutely one of the best experiences of my life.

These kids were clearly hard workers. Their work ethic was amazing. But their curiosity and their drive to intake as much information to make them a valuable tool in this world, it was very compelling. And I’m glad that I ended up doing that. In fact, when I do talk to veterans that come to Yale, I say, hey, man, get out of that comfort zone with the people that you’re accustomed to, the people that speak the same language and use the same acronyms. Spend some time with these kids.

The number one thing I learned from these kids was to just be ferocious about it. I feel like I was about that about everything else in my life. But here, I had there was some trepidation because I didn’t feel super comfortable. And then after a while, I was the 50 some-odd– I’d say, in your early 50s is where you start to not care what you wear. And you don’t really care if people think you’re silly or what. You don’t care as much. That helped me.

Also, some of the outreach that the kids did to me on Veterans Day, a young man who’s now– I think he’s prepping his law school applications. He’s a presidential scholar from Pennsylvania. I didn’t know what that was until I met him. On Veterans Day, he brought me a card. In that card, he had had the other kids in Directed Studies sign it and said, “Thank you for your service to our country.” And I was stunned.

And at that point, and some of the other younger people that I sat with and hung around with, I was in some of the same classes, we just became very close. In fact, we used to meet on Sundays over Zoom during COVID. And they’ve all spread to the wind. A couple of them were out at Stanford. One’s in law school. One’s getting his PhD in economics. But those relationships, I think I’ve touched what the undergraduates can touch.

Recently went to the class of ‘65 reunion. They made me an honorary member. And I saw these connections that these people had. They dispersed all over the world, but they’d come back together. And it was almost as if they’d never left each other. That type of relationship, I think, is really special. And I think I did not put a lot of weight on that. I wish that I had known that that was part of the education, as you referred to.

HANNAH: After the American withdrawal from Afghanistan, you proposed a course at the Jackson Institute for Global Affairs, and Yale kind of took you up on the idea. The course was led by former US ambassador to Pakistan, Anne Patterson. And you were sort of an unofficial coleader. Can you tell us a little bit about the goal for that class, why you proposed it, and what came out of it?

JIMMY HATCH: Actually, it’s funny, I didn’t actually propose it, but I was asked to go on Anderson’s show because they knew that I had been in Afghanistan, where I was wounded and that I’d lost friends. And I was pretty raw at that moment because of the way things were going with us leaving. And I remember saying– I was actually a little angry at the news. They’re talking about it in political terms. And on the program, I said, look, we need to do an after action.

And what I mean by that is, in the military, when we go out and do a mission, even if it’s completely successful, we come back, and we debrief it. And we tear it apart. We have to. If we don’t, we’re not going to evolve as a unit. And we’re going to fall behind. The enemy is going to have an advantage on us if we’re not critical of ourselves, if we can’t look each other in the eye and say, hey, man, this was a mistake. I felt like we needed to do that as a nation.

And one thing that I learned in the course with Ambassador Patterson was that we’d never done anything like that with Korea or Vietnam. There had been no specific group of people sit down, get the materials, drive through it, and come up with some after action suggestions even. So when I said that on CNN, a fellow who was running the American University in Kabul saw it and mentioned it to Dean Levinsohn. Dean Levinsohn messaged me. We talked, and he said, look, we’re going to do that.

Because on the program, I said, listen, we can’t have Congress do this after action because they can’t agree on facts. I thought the only place it could happen is in academia. It has to happen in academia. Because based on my experiences, specifically in Directed Studies, the raw, throw it out there, deal with it, I thought that’s exactly the kind of sunlight that this festering wound that we had as a nation needed to disinfect and heal so that we could then move on and make better decisions.

Look, some of the guys that I went to war with that were killed have kids, who’ve just recently graduated from the service academies or colleges, and they’re now serving in the military. And they deserve better decisions. They deserve to have people give themselves a hard look and say, hey, I messed this up. Don’t do this again. And I think in that Afghanistan class, I think we accomplished some very significant things. And it made me feel as if not only did we do an after action, but we ourselves, me in particular, changed significantly in our views about conflict.

MARK: So, Jimmy, one of the things that you did in this class was you invited a spokesperson for the Taliban to come and talk to the class.
