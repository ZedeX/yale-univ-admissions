MARK: Yes. Starting with this cycle, we will be requiring all first-year and transfer applicants to submit either the SAT or the ACT. This is a return to the testing policy that we had in place for a very long time, really, right up until 2020, when the pandemic changed things very quickly. 

HANNAH: So for four years, we had a totally test-optional policy, and then we had two years of a test-flexible policy. And under that flexible policy, applicants were required to submit testing. But we gave them four options– SAT, ACT, AP, or IB. And moving forward, that list of options is just going to be narrowed to just the SAT or the ACT. 

MARK: So about two years ago, we recorded several episodes all about our testing policy change. We did a big-picture episode. We did one that was about the details of that test-flexible policy, another one that kind of walked through the history of how we got there. And then there was a special bonus episode that had an elaborate baseball analogy. 

HANNAH: So much podcasting about testing. And everything we said in those episodes is still 100% true. The only thing that’s really changed since then is the number of options you’ll have when applying. You’ll have two instead of four. 

MARK: To save you some time, here’s a quick summary of the most important big-picture points we made about testing when we recorded those episodes back in 2024. 

HANNAH: I think the most common misconception people have about testing is simply to overestimate how much small differences in scores, one or two points on the ACT or 10 or 20 points on the SAT, matter in our process. 

MARK: Yeah, scores are important, but those small differences don’t tell us very much about you. It’s simply never the case that we would look at an application and say, well, if their ACT were 35 instead of a 34, or if they had a 1560 instead of a 1520, they’d be in. But because it’s just a little bit short, sorry, Charlie. 

HANNAH: Right. So we also run into the myth that there are these scores that get you in, scores that keep you out, and that there’s a specific break point. So if you’re above that, your score is good. And below that, your score is bad. 

MARK: Yeah. The first reason that this idea is wrong, or at least very incomplete, is that we look at test scores in terms of ranges. This is in line with how the tests are designed to be used. 

HANNAH: Yeah. And we also always evaluate test scores within an applicant’s broader context and alongside other academic indicators like high school transcript. 

MARK: Yeah. And of course, finally, our admissions process, the whole thing is designed to pick up on all of the very real and very significant differences, including differences in academic preparation, that exist between students who have equally strong scores. 

HANNAH: Right. So on their own, test scores certainly tell us something important. But they don’t tell us whether one applicant should be admitted and not tens of thousands of other applicants who have applied. 

MARK: Yeah. So just some important facts about our process that I think are relevant to share here too. We receive more applications from students who have perfect test scores, meaning a 1600 on the SAT or a 36 on the ACT, than we have spots in the first-year class. 

And let’s say that we extend it a little further and said, well, we think about this in terms of ranges. So you need at least a 1580 on the SAT or a 35 on the ACT. Well, if you did that, we would have more than four times as many applicants as we have spots. 

HANNAH: Right. So we don’t run an entirely test-based admissions process. We’re not outsourcing our jobs as admissions officers to testing agencies. But these standardized test scores add some very, very important data to an application. 

MARK: And that’s not just a matter of opinion among admissions officers. We have a lot of research to back that up. 

HANNAH: Yeah. Our whole selection process is designed to give us answers to lots of questions that we have about you. But the first question we always have, and the most important is, is this student academically prepared to succeed at Yale? 

MARK: Yes. And of the things that we have available to us, we think that standardized tests are the best tool that we have to answer that question. Now, we’re going to talk a lot more about what it means to be academically prepared to succeed at Yale generally, and how we admissions officers evaluate all that in our next episode. So stay tuned for more on this.

HANNAH: Yes. And we also want to mention that admissions officers know that prospective students don’t need perfect scores to succeed academically at Yale. But strong scores are very important. 

MARK: The overwhelming majority of Yale College students score above the 95th percentile on the SAT or the ACT. So for the most recent year that there is data, that was a 1420 on the SAT or 31 on the ACT. If you’ve scored in those ranges, good for you. You should be considering selective colleges like Yale. 

But it’s also worth noting here, just last year, as an example, about 60% of all of our applicants hit that benchmark, at least a 1420 or 31. So if you are within that group, we are still only able to select 1 out of every 16 applicants with those scores. 

HANNAH: Yeah. And it’s not the case that once you’re sort of above those numbers that the differences no longer matter anymore. We’re still looking at your individual test score. But remember, our selection process is also considering much more than just those scores to separate out that 1 student in 16, to make the difference between an acceptance and a denial. 

MARK: And to learn more about the things that separate students, listen to 50-plus episodes of our podcast, where we talk about all the other things. 

HANNAH: We love talking about those things. Yeah. 

MARK: OK. 

HANNAH: So you might be wondering, why are we making this change? Why are we making the change right now? 

MARK: The short answer here is that we are responding to a recommendation from Yale faculty members and leaders around the university. As we’ve talked about before, Yale’s internal research has shown very consistently that standardized test scores are the single greatest predictor of a student’s Yale academic performance. 

This is the big reason why we returned to requiring testing of all of our applicants two years ago. We simply felt, as admissions officers, that we were missing an important indicator for too many of our applicants. 

HANNAH: And we also found that applicants were kind of missing out on the opportunity to show their academic preparation. 

MARK: As admissions officers, we felt confident that we could pick up on that predictive signal from the SAT or the ACT, or AP or IB scores if we didn’t have those. And we have some research to back that up. But our colleagues around campus made it clear that they are more comfortable with the admissions office considering a narrower range of test results for greater consistency and transparency about what’s considered in our process. 

HANNAH: The other reason we’re going in this direction is that the overwhelming majority of applicants were already sending SAT or ACT scores, even with the flexible options. So in the first year of that test-flexible policy, about 3/4 of our applicants submitted SAT or ACT, which was up from only about half of our applicants when we were test-optional. And last year, that figure increased to almost 80%. 

MARK: And then if you look among our enrolling students, it’s been more than 90% of that group that have submitted the SAT or the ACT. We have been fans of the flexibility for all the reasons that we talked about on this podcast two years ago. But simplifying things is definitely, we think, a better reflection of what our applicants’ choices are and also our process. 

HANNAH: Of course, we also know one of the reasons why most of our applicants have been submitting the SAT or the ACT is that the national landscape has changed. Two years ago, Yale was one of the first highly selective universities to return to testing. And since then, most others have followed suit. Most have gone back to requiring the SAT or the ACT. 

MARK: So the good news for you in all of this is that we think that a narrower range of options will make things just simpler for you as an applicant, right? 

HANNAH: Yeah, absolutely. You won’t need to decide whether to apply with SAT or to use your AP scores instead. All you need to do is ensure that you’ve self-reported either the SAT or the ACT or both if you have them on your application. And if you also have AP scores or IB scores to share, go for it. There’s space in the application for those as well. 

DARA: And some friendly reminders– one, we don’t prefer the ACT to the SAT or vice versa. Yes, we superscore, though you shouldn’t create your own ACT composite superscore. That needs to come from the ACT. 

Also, you can choose which AP scores to include or exclude, although we do think it’s a good idea to share scores from any AP exam you completed because likely, you’ll beg some questions from the committee if you are obviously excluding a score. 

And finally, if you want to self-report a score that you didn’t include on your application, you can use the application update form that you’ll find on your Yale admission status portal after you submit your application. 

MARK: And of course, remember, folks, there’s lots more about testing and a little bit about baseball in those old testing episodes from 2024. Go give them a listen if you want to learn more. 

HANNAH: All right, so like Mark said, we plan to record another episode soon that goes even deeper into this idea of academic preparation. But before that, we do want to make sure that we talk to you a little bit about some changes to the writing questions on our application.

HANNAH: So last year for this episode, we talked a lot about one change we made to one question, which was the part of the application called “why Yale.” Most colleges have a question like this. It asks you to reflect on why you’ve decided to apply, what appeals to you about the college. 

And we made some changes last year to the wording of the question to try to help applicants be a little bit more reflective and to tell us something about themselves in that space, rather than something about Yale. 

MARK: Yes, we talked in that episode about how the responses to that question just hadn’t been very helpful over the years. And we hoped– especially, I hoped– that by changing the wording of this question to center it around reflection, that we would help applicants use that space better and tell us admissions officers more about themselves. 

HANNAH: Yeah. How’d it go? 

MARK: Not great, Hannah. Not great. 

HANNAH: The changes just didn’t quite work. We were still finding that most responses didn’t tell us anything new or interesting about the applicant. We still got a lot of what we had been getting before. 

MARK: Yes. 

HANNAH: Facts about Yale, name-dropping of professors and labs that we’d never even heard of, imagined, like, “here’s my day in the life at Yale” vignettes, and a lot of responses that demonstrated more about an applicant’s ability to search Yale and do research on Yale than about their personality, their interests, or the experiences that led them to apply. 

MARK: So when we see that tens of thousands of our applicants aren’t giving us what we hope to get out of an application question, we don’t just sit around and say, oh, those foolish students. They fell right into our trap. Why can’t they do better? 

Instead, we look inwards. And we say, well, gosh, that’s on us. We really need to rethink this question because it’s just not working. 

HANNAH: So we did that. As you know if you’ve listened to this podcast before, all the admissions officers get together every summer and think about how we’re asking these questions and if we’re getting what we want out of them. And this year, we ultimately decided to just drop this “why Yale” question completely. 

MARK: We are, of course, still very interested in understanding your fit for Yale. We want to see that your interests and your aspirations align with the Yale learning environment. But we think we can get all of that from other parts of the application. And we think connecting the dots between you and Yale is more our job than it is your job. You focus on telling us about yourself and use the spaces that we give you to do that. 

DARA: We also think that eliminating this question might help level the playing field ever so slightly between students who’ve had a lot of opportunities to connect with Yale before applying and those students who haven’t. 

HANNAH: Yeah. So that’s one less thing for you to write and one less part of the application for admissions officers to read. And you don’t need to worry about trying to squeeze in a “why Yale” to another part of the application. And in fact, please don’t use the space in that way. 

Applicants will often use the last paragraph of their Yale-specific essay to wrap it up and talk about why what they’ve written shows that they’re a great fit for Yale. But that is unnecessary. And it’s usually not a great use of that space, which is already quite limited. 

MARK: Yeah. Trust that what you’re writing about and how you’re writing about it can convey that sense of fit without you having to come right out and say, “and this is why I’m a great fit for Yale.” I like this saying that movie directors and theater directors use sometimes when they say, you can give the audience 2 and 2, and they can make 4. 

I think that when it comes to fit in a college application, you can do the same thing. You can tell us the things about yourself that you think are important for us to understand about your potential fit for the university. And we will recognize it and say, yes, I see that the student would be great for Yale. 

HANNAH: Right. But you can leave that work of drawing the line to us. 

MARK: Exactly. 

HANNAH: Yeah. OK. And then the other small change we made is to our short take questions. These are very brief, short-answer questions with a word limit of only about 35 words. We used to have four. We’re reducing the number from four to three. 

We took away two of the questions that we, as a staff, felt weren’t giving us the most revealing insights. And we added a new question, which I’m really excited about. What is one aspect of yourself that you hope to grow or develop during college?

HANNAH: I think one thing that we would sometimes get out of the “why Yale” question is this excitement that applicants had about what their college experience might be like and how they might grow or go in new, exciting directions during their college experience. 

So we decided to preserve that little piece of the “why Yale” and ask that question very directly, but not give you too much space to respond to it. 

DARA: And I think it’s really helpful for us to, again, get back to this fit question of do we have the things here that we think will help you grow and develop in the way that you want to once you leave our campus in four years. 

It is easier to do that than to say, here’s the day in the life of what I will be doing on campus, and here is who I’m going to be. I think it’s a much better thing to think about who you are now and how you want to get there. 

HANNAH: Yeah. And we like prompting this idea of the fact that you’re not coming to college fully formed and perfect, which I think is something that when you’re putting together your college applications, you feel like you have to be this perfectly packaged person. 

And hopefully, if you are a long-time listener of this podcast, you know that that’s not what we’re looking for. And one thing that we are really looking for is an openness to new experiences and growing and improving and all these things during college. So this question is an opportunity for us to remind you that that’s something that we care about, and for you to reflect on and think about what that might look for you. 

MARK: So another question– should I try to be really specific in this question about Yale-specific things that I want to engage with and that would help me? Should this be a place where I show off that I know particular parts of the Yale experience? 

HANNAH: I would say no. We don’t want this to be a one-for-one replacement to that “why Yale.” 

DARA: And you only have very limited space, so please use it wisely. I would say we don’t need those examples. Again, we want to hear about you. 

HANNAH: Yeah. So I would say when you’re approaching this question, be reflective. Just truly think about what we’re asking. What is something you hope you improve on during college? It can be a very big, serious thing. Or it could be something silly or something small. 

Feel free to go with whatever scale you feel tells us something new about you in the application. Maybe you want to really work on your communication skills and your writing, and you’re really excited to dive into that. Or maybe you just want to learn how to make new friends in a new environment. 

It could be something academic. It could be something social. It could be something else entirely. 

DARA: But if there is a natural place where your interest in Yale comes out, I would say you can include that in there if it feels like you’re not trying to make a plug here. 

HANNAH: As long as it’s still about you. 

MARK: And I think it’s good for folks to know that we didn’t put this here to basically say, and tell us about Yale in this space. We’re going to say college, but we really mean Yale. So we’ve talked about how the crafting of an application question from our side of things is a very deliberate and very thoughtful process. 

We will spend a lot of time thinking about each and every word. And so I’m even just looking at this on the page right now and analyzing it the way that you might analyze a piece of poetry. And so, well, just a couple of choices stand out to me that I want to draw our audience’s attention to. 

HANNAH: Sure. 

MARK: The first is we crafted the question in terms of “what is one aspect.” You didn’t say, what’s the most important aspect or the most significant aspect. There’s no superlatives here. It’s one. 

HANNAH: Just one.
